Example: "The tall boy quickly ran home." Quickly is an adverb because it tells how he ran.

2. Sentence types

By purpose

  • Declarative makes a statement: The bus arrives at 7.
  • Interrogative asks a question: When does the bus arrive?
  • Imperative gives a command or request: Close the door.
  • Exclamatory shows strong feeling: The bus is late again!

By structure

  • Simple: one independent clause. I studied.
  • Compound: two independent clauses joined by a conjunction. I studied, and she practiced.
  • Complex: an independent clause plus a dependent clause. I passed because I studied.

3. Commas

  1. In a list of three or more: We packed sandwiches, fruit, and water.
  2. Before a conjunction joining two independent clauses: I wanted to go, but it was raining.
  3. After an introductory phrase: After the game, we got pizza.
  4. Around extra information that could be removed: My brother, who lives in Ohio, is visiting.

A handy memory trick for the conjunctions in rule 2 is FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.

5. Writing a paragraph

  1. Topic sentence: states the main idea.
  2. Evidence: facts, examples, or quotes that support it.
  3. Explanation: says why the evidence matters.
  4. Concluding sentence: wraps up the idea.
Example: Reading every day improves vocabulary. Students who read for twenty minutes a night meet far more new words than those who do not. Seeing those words in context helps them stick. Daily reading is one of the simplest ways to build a stronger vocabulary.
